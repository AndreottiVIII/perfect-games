Every game starts out perfect. After 9 batters, 1 in 32 still is. After 27, just 1 in 17,457.

A perfect game is one where none of the 27 opposing batters reaches base: 27 up, 27 down. Every game begins that way. For each team in the field (the pitching side) in every regular-season game from 1908 to 2025, we count how many opposing batters it retires in a row before the first one reaches base.

Still perfect: 1 game in… after each batter, inning by inning

Read it like a line score: each inning, the 1st, 2nd and 3rd batter up. Yellow = once, twice and three times through the lineup.

  • Out of 402,184 pitching sides, the game is still perfect after 9 batters (once through the lineup) 1 time in 32; after 18, 1 in 826; after 27, 1 in 17,457.
  • On the log scale the curve is almost a straight line: with every batter the perfect game loses roughly the same share, about a third. Why is on the How it falls page.
  • The eras look a lot alike. In the pitchers’ years (1961-1972) and since 2010, 1 game in 27 is still perfect after 9 batters; between 1920 and 1941, when teams scored more, 1 in 39.
  • By batter 27 there are hardly any cases left: 21 perfect games in 118 seasons (20 official ones plus Galarraga’s, see The 21 games). That is why the era lines stop earlier, once fewer than 30 sides are still perfect.

The numbers behind the chart
How we worked it out

Source: Retrosheet’s “event files”, the play-by-play of each game. We use the regular season of the American League, the National League and the Federal League (1914-1915): two rows per game, one for each pitching side, 402,184 in all.

We leave out games that Retrosheet rebuilt from the final box score rather than from a real play-by-play, and the Negro Leagues.

If a game ends before anyone reaches base (a rain-shortened game, or a home team that wins without batting in the ninth), that side counts as still perfect up to the last batter it faced and then drops out of the count (the Kaplan-Meier method).

The figure at 27 batters (1 in 17,457) also includes Harvey Haddix (1959) and Pedro Martínez (1995), who got past 27 outs but lost the perfect game in extra innings: 23 cases in all.

Armando Galarraga’s game (June 2, 2010) counts as perfect by Davide’s choice: umpire Jim Joyce blew the call on the 27th batter and admitted it afterwards, but MLB does not recognise it as a perfect game.

Eras: instead of 12 nearly identical decades we use seven periods that match changes in the game. The dead ball up to 1919 (few home runs); the live ball from 1920; the war and postwar years from 1942; expansion and the pitchers’ years from 1961; the DH in the American League from 1973; expansion and the home run years from 1993; the strikeout years from 2010.
