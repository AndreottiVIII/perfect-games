Ten things hidden in the data, from the most solid to the least.

Each item has its number and a verdict: solid holds up under statistical checks; to verify is interesting but the sample is small, or we found it by looking at the data; still open we checked hard and still can’t explain; anecdote is true but may be chance.

  1. The last out is nearly always a pinch hitter solid

    In 13 of the 20 official perfect games (65%) the last man retired was a pinch hitter, a substitute sent up to bat for a teammate. In the 12 games where the perfect game fell apart at the 27th batter, the man who broke it up was a pinch hitter 9 times. At the 9th batter, by contrast, a pinch hitter shows up only 0.9% of the time.

    The reason is simple: in the ninth inning the losing team sends up a substitute. Pinch hitters are no more dangerous than anyone else (9 of 22 reached base, against 3 of 10): what counts is that they are there, not how good they are.

  2. Two ballparks really change the odds: Coors Field and Fenway Park solid

    At Coors Field (Denver) starts with at least 9 batters retired in a row number 105 against 169 expected (−38%), and in 4,900 pitching sides there is not a single run of 18 (6.1 were expected). At Fenway Park (Boston), 411 against 521 (−21%). At the other end, Dodger Stadium: 443 against 382 (+16%), the most pitcher-friendly of today’s parks; among the old ones, the Polo Grounds in New York (+27%).

    We checked again with the even/odd rule from the Torture test: Coors Field, Fenway Park, the Polo Grounds and Arlington all point the same way, clearly, in both even and odd seasons. Details in Ballparks.

  3. Specialists exist solid

    If all pitchers were alike, 4.1 of them would have at least 5 starts with 15 or more batters retired in a row: there are 17. Max Scherzer and Nolan Ryan lead (7 starts), then Randy Johnson, Mark Buehrle and Yu Darvish (6; Darvish in only 297 starts, where 1.2 were expected).

  4. More strikeouts, but no fewer pitches solid

    The 20 official perfect games average 9.3 strikeouts against 6.5 expected in the same season: +43%. But pitches per batter are the same as in other runs of outs: 3.97 against 3.90. Since 1988 the average is 107 pitches a game, from 88 for David Cone to 125 for Matt Cain. The last out is a strikeout 7 times out of 20.

  5. What doesn’t matter: umpire, day or night, month, weather, crowd solid

    These are the factors people mention most, and nothing shows up: the home-plate umpire, day or night, the month, the day of the week, the size of the crowd, wind, rain, sky, a wet field. One exception turned up later: temperature. Here, split into four bands, it looked like noise; tested on every plate appearance in the Torture test, hotter days bring slightly more baserunners.

  6. Deep into a perfect game, pitchers don’t give away walks solid

    When a perfect game ends at batters 1 to 9, it is a walk 25.7% of the time; at batters 10 to 18, 21.0%; at 19 to 27, just 17.0%. Fielding errors go the other way, up to 6.5%. It is not the bottom of the order or pinch hitters, and only a sliver of it is the pitcher’s usual control. It shows up in even and odd seasons alike (in the odd half alone the late drop just misses the usual bar). Details in the Torture test.

  7. When a perfect game dies without a hit, sometimes the no-hitter survives solid

    A no-hitter is a game with no hits allowed, even if someone reaches base. Of 121 complete games in which a run of at least 18 outs ended on a walk, hit by pitch, error or interference, 16 (13%) still ended as no-hitters. The three breaks at the 27th batter without a hit (Hooks Wiltse 1908, Milt Pappas 1972, Max Scherzer 2015) all ended as no-hitters. If the break is a hit, the no-hitter never survives (0 of 346).

  8. Six perfect games in three years, then one in thirteen still open

    In 2010 (Dallas Braden, Roy Halladay and Armando Galarraga) and 2012 (Philip Humber, Matt Cain, Félix Hernández): 6 of the 21 in three seasons. Luck puts 6 or more into some three-season window about 1% of the time by our count (about 5.5% without Galarraga); an independent reviewer got about 0.4% (2.9%). And 2010-12 was not a pitcher-friendly stretch on the rest of the curve. Unusual, unexplained, possibly luck: the full story is in the Torture test.

  9. The 12 who stopped one batter short anecdote

    Six singles, two doubles, two hit by pitch, one walk and one home run (Ken Phelps off Brian Holman, 1990). The two hit batters (Wiltse 1908 and Scherzer 2015) make 17%, against 1.9% of all breaks: curious, but we noticed it after the fact. Full list in The 21 games.

  10. Victims and ballparks of the 21 perfect games anecdote

    Tampa Bay has been on the wrong end of 3 perfect games (Buehrle 2009, Braden 2010, Hernández 2012) and the Oakland Coliseum has hosted 3 (Catfish Hunter, Braden, Domingo Germán). It looks strange but isn’t: some team taking 3 of 21 by chance happens 65% of the time, some ballpark hosting 3, 38%.

How we worked it out

The games are the 20 official perfect games found in the data (1908-2025), plus Galarraga’s by Davide’s choice (MLB does not recognise it), and the 12 times the perfect game ended at exactly the 27th batter.

For ballparks, umpires, months and weather the “expected” figures are the share of sides with at least k batters retired in a row in the same season and with the same kind of lineup (DH or not), added up over the sides in the group. With many comparisons (294 umpires, for example) some big gaps turn up by chance: that is why the results are corrected for the number of comparisons.

Pinch hitter: position 11 in Retrosheet’s play-by-play. Pitches: reliably counted only since 1988.
